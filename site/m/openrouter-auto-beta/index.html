You might think of cyberattacks and hacking as a modern phenomenon tied to computers and the internet. But the first recorded "cyberattack" actually happened in 1903, decades before the first computer was ever built.

It all started with Guglielmo Marconi, the inventor of the wireless telegraph. In June 1903, Marconi was set to give a highly publicized public demonstration of his new wireless communication system at the Royal Institution in London. He claimed his system was completely secure and immune to interference, meaning private messages could be sent without fear of being intercepted.

A rival inventor and magician named Nevil Maskelyne disagreed. To prove Marconi wrong, Maskelyne set up a 50-meter radio mast near the theater and waited for the demonstration to begin.

As Marconi's assistant began sending the "secure" Morse code transmission from a remote location to the theater, Maskelyne hijacked the signal. Suddenly, the receiver in the theater started tapping out a different message. Maskelyne sent a cheeky poem mocking Marconi:

"There was a young fellow of Italy, who diddled the public quite prettily."

The demonstration was completely derailed. Maskelyne later wrote a letter to The Times admitting to the hack, stating he did it purely to show the public that Marconi's claims of absolute security were false. Thus, the first wireless hack—and arguably the first cyberattack—was pulled off by a magician using a giant radio antenna.

For centuries, a popular shade of paint used by famous artists was literally made from human remains.

Between the 16th and 20th centuries, European artists used a rich, warm pigment known as "Mummy Brown." The secret ingredient? Ground-up ancient Egyptian mummies.

It was highly prized for its transparency and depth, and was used by notable artists like Eugène Delacroix and the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood. Originally, apothecaries in the Middle Ages sold ground-up mummy powder as a "miracle medicine." When people eventually realized eating mummies wasn't doing them any favors, merchants found themselves with a surplus of imported Egyptian mummies. To avoid taking a loss, they sold them to paintmakers to be ground into a fine pigment.

The practice finally died out in the mid-20th century. This was partly because artists became disturbed upon learning exactly what they were painting with, and partly because the supply of mummies had finally dwindled to the point of being unobtainable.
