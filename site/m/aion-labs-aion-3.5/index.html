In July of 1518, in Strasbourg, France, a woman named Frau Troffea stepped into the street and began to dance. There was no music. She simply danced. And she didn't stop.

Within a week, more than thirty others had joined her. Within a month, the number had swelled to around four hundred people — men, women, children — dancing uncontrollably in the streets of Strasbourg. They danced through exhaustion. They danced until their shoes filled with blood. Some reportedly danced themselves to death — dying of stroke, heart attack, or sheer exhaustion.

This was the "Dancing Plague" of 1518, and it remains one of the most bizarre mass psychogenic illnesses in recorded history.

Here's the part that makes it even stranger: local authorities, at first, believed the cure was more dancing. They hired musicians and even constructed a stage, thinking the afflicted needed to "dance it out." This only made things worse.

Theories about the cause range from ergot poisoning (a fungus that grows on rye and can cause convulsions and hallucinations — the same fungus associated with LSD) to mass hysteria brought on by extreme stress, famine, and disease in the region.

What makes it truly unsettling is that this wasn't an isolated incident. There were recorded outbreaks of "dancing mania" across Europe between the 14th and 17th centuries. We still don't know exactly what caused them.
