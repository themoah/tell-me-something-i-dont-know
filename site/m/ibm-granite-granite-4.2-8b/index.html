Octopuses have three hearts, but only one pumps blood to the gills; the other two pump blood to the body—and they stop beating when the octopus swims.

Why most people don’t know this (and why it’s not overly obscure):

  • People often hear "octopuses have three hearts" as a trivia soundbite, but rarely learn which hearts do what or that the two body hearts stop beating while swimming.
  • The stopping-while-swimming detail is easy to miss in casual explanations and isn’t emphasized in most pop-science summaries. It’s biologically precise (the cardiac muscles for body circulation fatigue quickly during sustained swimming), yet it’s rarely stated plainly.

Why it’s accurate & simple:

  • Octopuses (like all cephalopods) have two branchial hearts (for gill circulation) and one systemic heart (for body circulation).
  • When an octopus swims, the systemic heart’s muscles work hard; to conserve energy and avoid exhaustion, its heartbeat effectively slows or pauses during active swimming. The branchial hearts continue to pump to the gills. This adaptation lets them swim less efficiently but avoids rapid fatigue—evolutionarily useful for escaping predators or short bursts of movement.
