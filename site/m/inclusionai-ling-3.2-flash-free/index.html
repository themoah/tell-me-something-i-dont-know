The "Oh-My-God Particle"

On October 15, 1991, the Fly's Eye cosmic ray detector in Dugway, Utah, recorded a single subatomic particle — most likely a proton — traveling at roughly 99.99999999999999999999951% the speed of light.

It carried about 3 × 10²⁰ electron volts of energy. To put that in perspective: that's the kinetic energy of a baseball moving at 60 mph — compressed into a single proton. If you could somehow ride alongside it, due to relativistic time dilation, it would experience the entire age of the universe (13.8 billion years) as passing in roughly 16 days from its own frame of reference.

The particle became known as the "Oh-My-God particle" because, when physicists first calculated its energy, their reaction was essentially that.

What makes it truly baffling: according to the Greisen–Zatsepin–Kuzmin (GZK) limit, protons with that much energy should lose energy rapidly through interactions with the cosmic microwave background radiation over distances greater than about 160 million light-years. So whatever produced it had to be relatively "nearby" in cosmic terms — yet no obvious source (no nearby active galactic nucleus, no suitable supernova remnant) has ever been identified in the direction it came from.

It remains one of the great unsolved mysteries in astrophysics. Similar ultra-high-energy cosmic rays have been detected since (the Telescope Array and Pierre Auger Observatory have recorded a handful), but we still don't know what can accelerate a particle to that speed.
