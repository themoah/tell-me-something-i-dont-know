Here's one of my favorites, because it's a case where scientists got to watch something happen that they thought they'd never see: a language being born from scratch.

Before the 1970s, Nicaragua had no deaf community to speak of. Deaf children were raised in isolation from one another, each improvising crude "home signs" with their families. Then in 1977 the country opened its first large school for deaf children, followed by a vocational school. The teachers focused on lip-reading and Spanish, which mostly failed. But out on the playground and on the buses, a few hundred kids who had never had peers suddenly did — and they started pooling their homemade gestures.

The first cohort produced something like a pidgin: functional, inconsistent, not much grammar. The interesting part is what the younger children did with it. Kids who arrived at the school and learned this rough system from the older students didn't just copy it — they regularized it. They invented consistent grammatical machinery: spatial agreement, systematic verb structure, ways to mark who did what to whom. Within roughly two decades, Nicaraguan Sign Language became a full, complex language, and each new generation of children made it more sophisticated than the input they'd received.

Judy Kegl, an American linguist, arrived in 1986 and realized what she was looking at. It's now one of the strongest pieces of evidence that children don't merely absorb language — they actively impose structure on it, even when the structure isn't there to begin with. The learners were the creators.

There's a poignant footnote: the oldest members of the community, who missed the critical window, never became fully fluent. Researchers found they struggle with concepts like tracking what someone else falsely believes — suggesting language may be scaffolding for certain kinds of thought, not just a way to report it.

Here's one that surprises most people who've absorbed the standard science-class version:

The Earth has recently been spinning faster, and we may soon need to subtract a second from our clocks for the first time in history.

The textbook story is that tidal friction from the Moon steadily slows Earth's rotation — about 1.8 milliseconds added to the length of a day per century, with the Moon drifting away from us roughly 3.8 cm a year. That's why we've had to insert "leap seconds" into official time, 27 of them since 1972.

But since around 2020, the trend reversed. Earth started spinning slightly quicker than the standard 86,400-second day, and July 5, 2024 came in about 1.66 milliseconds short — one of the shortest days ever measured. No leap second has been added since the end of 2016. If this continues, timekeepers project we'll need a negative leap second — literally skipping a second — around 2029, which has never been done and which a lot of software is unprepared for.

Two nice wrinkles:

  • Nobody fully knows why. The best suspect is the sloshing of Earth's liquid outer core exchanging angular momentum with the mantle above it.
  • Melting polar ice is having the opposite effect. Moving all that mass from the poles toward the equator flattens the planet slightly, which slows rotation — like a figure skater extending their arms. One estimate is that human-driven ice melt has already postponed that negative leap second by about three years. Climate change is, in a small and literal way, buying us time.

Meanwhile, the international community voted in 2022 to phase leap seconds out entirely by 2035, letting atomic time and Earth time drift apart for decades before making a bigger correction.

Here's one of my favorites: there's a wild bird in sub-Saharan Africa that actively recruits humans as hunting partners, and the two species have worked out a shared vocabulary for it.

The greater honeyguide (Indicator indicator) eats beeswax, but it can't break into a bees' nest on its own. Humans can, but they're bad at finding nests in dense bush. So honey-hunters among the Yao people in Mozambique make a specific call — a rolling trill followed by a grunt, roughly "brrrr-hm" — and honeyguides come to them, then fly ahead in short hops, chattering and waiting, leading them tree by tree to a nest. The hunters smoke out the bees and take the honey; the bird gets the wax and larvae.

The remarkable part is that this is a two-way signaling system, not just humans exploiting a bird's habits. In an experiment published in 2016, researchers played the traditional call versus control sounds while walking with hunters, and the specific call roughly doubled the chance of being guided and roughly tripled the overall success rate of finding a nest. Later work showed the birds respond preferentially to the call used by their local human culture — Mozambican honeyguides prefer the Yao trill-grunt, Tanzanian ones prefer the Hadza's melodic whistle. Two separate traditions, one on each side of the partnership.

And here's the detail that makes it strange: honeyguides are brood parasites. They lay their eggs in other birds' nests, and the chicks kill their foster siblings with hooks on their bills. So a honeyguide never meets its parents. Whatever it knows about cooperating with humans, it did not learn from its own kind's family life — which leaves an unresolved question about how the behavior is transmitted at all.

Some researchers think this relationship could be tens of thousands of years old, making it one of the last surviving examples of a kind of mutualism that may once have been more common between humans and wild animals.
