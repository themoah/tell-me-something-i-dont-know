Here's one: The wood frog (Rana sylvatica) can survive being completely frozen solid.

When winter comes, this small amphibian lets ice crystals form inside its body — in its skin, muscles, even around its organs. Its heart stops beating. Its blood freezes. It stops breathing. By all biological measures, it's dead.

But then, when temperatures rise in spring, it thaws out — its heart starts again, its blood flows, and it hops away as if nothing happened.

How? It survives by flooding its cells with glucose and a special protein called uromodulin, which acts as biological antifreeze. These substances prevent the ice from rupturing cell membranes — essentially turning the frog into a living popsicle that can reboot itself.

Scientists are still studying this process to advance organ preservation and cryopreservation in humans. So the next time you feel cold, just remember: there's a frog out there that literally lets it freeze.

Here's one that tends to surprise people:

A day on Venus is longer than a year on Venus.

Venus takes about 243 Earth days to complete a single rotation on its axis (a "day"), but only about 225 Earth days to orbit the Sun (a "year"). So a single sunrise-to-sunrise cycle on Venus is actually longer than the entire time it takes to go around the Sun once.

And to make it even weirder — Venus rotates backwards (retrograde rotation) compared to most planets in our solar system. If you could stand on Venus, the Sun would rise in the west and set in the east. Also, due to its extremely slow rotation combined with its thick atmosphere, the solar day on Venus is actually about 116.75 Earth days — but the sidereal day (true rotation period) remains the longer of the two compared to its orbital period.
