Here's one that surprises most people:

Bananas can set off nuclear radiation detectors at border crossings.

It's true. Bananas are naturally radioactive — they contain potassium-40, a radioactive isotope of potassium. While the amount is harmless (you'd need to eat roughly 10 million bananas in one sitting to be in any danger), the radiation is real enough to be measurable. In fact, a single banana can cause a Geiger counter to click slightly more.

This has actually caused real-world problems: shipping containers full of bananas have triggered radiation sensors at ports and border security checkpoints, leading to false alarms and unnecessary inspections. There's even a unit of measurement called the "Banana Equivalent Dose" (BED), used by physicists to put tiny radiation exposures into relatable context.

So the next time someone tells you to avoid radiation, just remind them that nature put a little bit of it in your fruit bowl. 🍌

Here's one that might surprise you:

Lake Natron in Tanzania turns animals into stone-like sculptures.

The lake's water is extremely alkaline — with a pH as high as 10.5, comparable to ammonia. It's heated by volcanic activity beneath it and saturated with sodium carbonate and other minerals. When birds or bats fly over the lake and accidentally land on its surface, the high alkalinity essentially calcifies their bodies, preserving them in eerie, lifelike poses that look like stone statues.

The photographer Nick Brandt captured these preserved creatures in haunting photos for his book Across the Ravaged Land, giving them a dignified portrait-like presentation. It's one of the most visually striking and biologically strange natural phenomena on Earth.

The lake is also the only known regular breeding ground for around 2.5 million lesser flamingos — they thrive there precisely because the caustic water keeps predators away.
