Here’s the play-by-play (from a 2018 Université de Montréal study):

  1. The wasp injects a ladybug with one egg plus a polydnavirus (PDV) grown in its own ovary (this virus is evolutionarily fused to the wasp’s genome, so it’s not a random pathogen).
  2. The larva hatches and eats only non-vital ladybug tissue (fat bodies, reproductive organs—never the gut, main nerve cord, or visual lobes) to keep its host alive, using ~50% of the ladybug’s mass max.
  3. When ready to pupate, the larva chews a tiny hole out of the ladybug’s abdomen, spins a cocoon between the ladybug’s legs, and the PDV flips a final switch: it blocks the neurotransmitter acetylcholine exclusively in the ladybug’s ventral motor neurons—so the ladybug CANNOT move its legs, wings, or body to escape.
  4. The secret, unpublicized twist: The PDV cannot cross the ladybug’s blood-brain barrier to reach its optic lobes. So the ladybug can still see moving objects (hungry chickadees, praying mantises) and trigger a tiny, involuntary flinch (a reflex from its partially functional nervous system)—which scares off predators that would eat the wasp’s cocoon.
  5. Bonus gut punch: ~30% of ladybugs survive this. Once the adult wasp emerges, the PDV breaks down, the ladybug regains motor control, and can even go back to eating aphids—though its reproductive system is usually too damaged to breed.

Viral science content almost always skips the virus’s hyper-specific neural targeting (the "sighted bodyguard" angle) to frame ladybugs as mindless zombies—this granular, mechanism-driven detail is the stuff most people never pick up.
