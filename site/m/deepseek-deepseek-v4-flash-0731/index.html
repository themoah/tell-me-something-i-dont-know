Here are five facts that are likely to be entirely new to you, spanning different fields of science, history, and biology. I've aimed for the ones that are the most counterintuitive or that debunk common misconceptions.

1. The Amazon rainforest does not produce 20% of the world's oxygen (and never did).
You've probably heard this statistic your whole life. It's a myth. The Amazon produces about 6–9% of the oxygen in the atmosphere. The real oxygen powerhouse is the ocean—specifically, microscopic phytoplankton that produce between 50% and 80% of the Earth's oxygen. The "20% from the Amazon" figure was a misunderstanding of a 1980s study about how much oxygen is recycled by the forest, not produced for the atmosphere.

2. Sloths can hold their breath longer than dolphins.
Dolphins can stay submerged for about 10 to 15 minutes. Sloths—those famously slow, tree-dwelling mammals—can hold their breath for up to 40 minutes underwater. They achieve this by slowing their heart rate to one-third of its normal speed and shunting blood away from their limbs to their vital organs. They do this to cross rivers or escape predators, making them surprisingly adept swimmers.

3. You are technically "blind" every time you move your eyes.
This is called saccadic masking. When your eyes dart from one focal point to another (which happens several times per second), your brain actively blocks the visual signal from your retinas. This prevents you from seeing a blurry smear of motion. During those fractions of a second, you are completely blind, but your brain fills in the gaps with what it expects to see, so you never notice. It's the reason you don't see the world spinning when you look around.

4. The can opener was invented 48 years after the tin can.
The tin can was patented in 1810, but the first dedicated can opener wasn't invented until 1858. For nearly half a century, how did people open cans? They used a hammer and chisel, or sometimes a bayonet. The cans were so thick that the instructions on early cans literally said: "Cut round the top near the outer edge with a chisel and hammer." The modern rotating can opener didn't appear until 1870.

5. A "jiffy" is a real, measurable unit of time in physics.
In everyday language, a jiffy means "a moment." But in physics, a jiffy is a precise unit defined as the time it takes light to travel one centimeter in a vacuum. That works out to approximately 33.3564 picoseconds (or 0.000000000033 seconds). In electronics, a jiffy is also used to refer to the time between alternating current power cycles.

Did any of these surprise you, or did you already know one? If you knew all of them, I can dig even deeper!

Here are three things you likely don't know, spanning biology, botany, and linguistics.

1. A sloth can hold its breath longer than a dolphin. We think of sloths as slow, land-bound tree dwellers, but they are surprisingly adept swimmers. When they cross rivers or navigate flooded forests, they can slow their heart rate to about one-third of its normal speed and hold their breath for up to 40 minutes. In contrast, a bottlenose dolphin, a marine mammal built for diving, typically only holds its breath for about 10 to 15 minutes before needing to surface.

2. Some bamboo species flower once every 120 years—and every single plant on Earth does it at the exact same time. This is called "gregarious flowering." Certain species of bamboo, like Phyllostachys bambusoides, live as clones for decades, spreading underground. Then, for reasons scientists still don't fully understand, a biological clock triggers them to flower. When it happens, every single plant of that species across the entire globe—whether it's in Japan, the UK, or the US—blooms within the same year. After flowering, the bamboo produces seeds and then dies. This is so synchronized that it can cause ecological chaos, as animals that eat the seeds experience a massive population boom, and the bamboo forests suddenly vanish.

3. The word "muscle" comes from the Latin word for "little mouse." The ancient Romans named our body tissue musculus, which is a diminutive of mus (mouse). They thought the flexing of a bicep or calf muscle looked like a little mouse running around under the skin. This etymology is also why a "muscle mouse" (a computer mouse) is a weirdly circular name—the computer mouse was named because it looked like the animal, and the animal's name was used for the body part centuries earlier.
