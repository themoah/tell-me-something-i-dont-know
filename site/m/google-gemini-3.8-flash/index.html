Humans have a notoriously weak sense of smell compared to other animals, with one massive exception: we are better at smelling rain than sharks are at smelling blood in water.

The distinctive, earthy scent that occurs when rain hits dry ground is called petrichor, caused largely by a microbial compound called geosmin.

The human nose can detect geosmin at concentrations as low as 5 to 10 parts per trillion. To put that into perspective, that is the equivalent of detecting a single teaspoon of a chemical dissolved in 200 Olympic-sized swimming pools. By comparison, a shark's legendary ability to smell blood maxes out at roughly one part per million to one part per billion.

Evolutionary biologists believe we developed this hypersensitivity because our early ancestors needed to track weather patterns across arid landscapes; being able to smell rain miles away meant the difference between finding fresh water and migrating game or dying of dehydration.

For about 50 million years, dead trees simply did not rot.

During the Carboniferous period (around 300 to 360 million years ago), plants evolved a miraculous new organic compound: lignin. It’s the rigid, tough polymer that allows bark to form, giving trees the structural strength to grow tall and reach for the sunlight.

The catch? Nature had not yet developed a way to decompose it.

No bacteria, fungus, or insect possessed the digestive enzymes required to break down lignin. So, when these towering, prehistoric trees died, they didn’t decay. They just fell over and piled up on top of each other—layer upon layer, for millions of years. Massive, global log-jams miles deep covered the planet.

Because they didn't rot, the carbon inside the wood was never re-released into the atmosphere as carbon dioxide. Instead, the crushing weight of the earth compressed this staggering pile of dead matter over eons.

This single 50-million-year period created virtually all of the coal deposits on Earth today.

Eventually, a group of fungi evolved the specialized enzymes (specifically, white-rot fungi) capable of breaking down lignin, and the logjam ended. Because of this, Earth will never naturally produce vast new coal fields ever again—anything that dies today gets eaten and recycled back into the atmosphere long before it can become fossilized fuel.

Ever wonder why artificial banana flavor (like in Runts, Laffy Taffy, or popsicles) tastes almost nothing like the bananas you buy at the grocery store?

It’s not because the chemical companies did a bad job—it’s because it was modeled after a banana that practically no longer exists.

Until the 1950s, the world’s dominant commercial banana was a variety called the Gros Michel (or "Big Mike"). It was richer, sweeter, and had a much higher concentration of isoamyl acetate—the exact chemical compound used to make artificial banana flavoring. When chemists first synthesized banana flavoring in the mid-19th century, they based it on the Gros Michel, because that was simply what a banana tasted like to people back then.

However, the Gros Michel was virtually wiped out worldwide in the 1950s by a soil fungus known as Panama Disease.

To save their businesses, fruit companies scrambled to replace it and settled on the Cavendish banana—the one you see in supermarkets today. The Cavendish was immune to the fungus, but it was blander, less fragrant, and had a different chemical profile than its predecessor.

So, every time you eat a piece of banana-flavored candy, you are essentially tasting a ghost from the 19th century: a flavor memory of an era of fruit that modern humans almost never get to experience.
