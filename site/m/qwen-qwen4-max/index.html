Here's one: the smell of rain is a bacterial advertisement, and it isn't meant for you.

That earthy scent after rain on dry ground is mostly geosmin, a compound made by Streptomyces bacteria living in soil. Humans are freakishly sensitive to it — we can detect it at a few parts per trillion. You'd notice a teaspoon stirred into a few hundred Olympic swimming pools.

For a long time geosmin was assumed to be metabolic exhaust — just a waste product that happened to smell. But a 2020 study found it works as a lure. Springtails, tiny soil arthropods, follow the scent to the bacteria and eat them. The spores survive: they stick to the springtail's water-repellent cuticle, and the ones that get eaten pass through the gut still viable, deposited in droppings somewhere new. The bacteria get eaten, but their offspring get a free ride — essentially the same bargain a berry makes with a bird.

So the smell isn't a byproduct. It's marketing, refined over hundreds of millions of years, aimed at an animal a millimeter long.

And we're out here inhaling it and writing poetry about it, eavesdropping on a conversation that predates flowers.

Here's one that surprised me: the smell of rain is a bacterial advertisement.

The molecule responsible is called geosmin (literally "earth smell"), and humans are freakishly sensitive to it — we can detect it at around five parts per trillion. That's roughly a teaspoon stirred into 400 Olympic swimming pools. We're better at smelling this one compound than we are at smelling almost anything else.

But geosmin isn't produced by rain, or wet stone, or soil chemistry. It's made by Streptomyces, a genus of soil bacteria. And for a long time nobody knew why they bothered — it's metabolically expensive to make a smell.

In 2020 a team figured it out. Springtails — tiny soil arthropods — follow the scent to its source and eat the bacteria. The bacterial spores survive being eaten, and get carried off in the springtails' droppings or stuck to their bodies. The smell is a come-hither signal to a dispersal agent. Same reason fruit smells good.

Two footnotes I like:

Geosmin is also why beets taste earthy, why catfish sometimes tastes muddy, and why your tap water occasionally smells like a basement. It's the same molecule every time.

And Streptomyces is where we got most of our antibiotics — streptomycin, tetracycline, erythromycin, and dozens more. So the smell you associate with fresh air and clean rain is, quite literally, the smell of the organisms that made modern medicine possible.
