Here’s a fact that sounds impossible but is 100% true:

Octopuses are colorblind—yet they are the masters of camouflage.

How does that work?

Their skin contains specialized cells called chromatophores—tiny, elastic sacs filled with pigment (yellow, red, brown, black). The octopus controls these sacs with muscles, stretching them to flash colors or shrinking them to disappear. Beneath those lie iridophores and leucophores, structural layers that reflect light to create shimmering blues, greens, and silvers—colors the octopus cannot actually "see" in the normal sense.

But here’s the kicker: Their skin can sense light independently of their eyes.

Research has shown that octopus skin contains light-sensitive proteins (opsins) similar to those found in their eyes. This means their skin can literally "see" changes in light and adjust its color and texture locally—even if the octopus is blindfolded or in the dark. They don’t need to consciously match their surroundings; their skin does it automatically, at a level below conscious thought.

So while an octopus may not perceive a rainbow the way we do, its skin is a living, breathing, color-sensing canvas—one that can change in milliseconds to vanish into nearly any background.
