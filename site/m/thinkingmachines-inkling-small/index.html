Here’s one that tends to stump even well-read people:

The “immortal” jellyfish, Turritopsis dohrnii, can revert to its juvenile form after reaching adulthood and live that cycle over and over again—potentially forever.

When it’s stressed, injured, or starving, this tiny jellyfish doesn’t just die; it transforms its mature cells back into a younger state through a process called transdifferentiation. It essentially turns back into a polyp (the sessile, baby-like stage), then grows up again, and can repeat this indefinitely. Because of that, it doesn’t appear to age or die of old age; it only dies if something eats it or a disease gets it.

Most animals have a fixed lifespan because their cells accumulate damage and lose the ability to regenerate. This jellyfish can reset that clock, which is why biologists call it “biologically immortal.”

(If you already knew that, try this one: The inventor of the Frisbee, Walter Frederick Morrison, was cremated, and his ashes were molded into Frisbee discs that were thrown at his memorial service.)

I can’t literally know what you don’t know, but here’s a fact that tends to survive even in well-read people:

Octopuses have nine brains, and about two-thirds of their neurons live in their arms—not their head.

Each arm has its own “arm-brain” (a cluster of neurons) that can taste, touch, and make decisions independently. If you sever an octopus arm (morbid, I know), it can still grasp objects, avoid pain, and react to stimuli for over an hour because the local neural network is that self-contained.

What this means in practice is that an octopus is less like a human controlling a body and more like a CEO setting broad goals while eight semi-autonomous operations managers run the show. When it’s exploring a coral crevice, the central brain isn’t micromanaging every suction-cup movement; the arms are basically “thinking” for themselves, deciding whether to pass something to the mouth or investigate further.

So intelligence doesn’t have to be centralized, and “brain” doesn’t have to mean “one thing in a skull.”

If you already knew that—fair, you’re probably smarter than me—but if not, it’s a pretty good reminder that evolution can build cognition in ways that look nothing like ours.
