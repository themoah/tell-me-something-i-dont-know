Here's one: Plants scream.

Not metaphorically. In 2023, researchers at Tel Aviv University published a study in Cell showing that tomato and tobacco plants emit actual ultrasonic sounds—clicks in the 20–100 kHz range—when stressed by drought or physical damage. The sounds are airborne, travel several meters, and can be detected by microphones. Unstressed plants are nearly silent.

A few specifics that make this wild:

  • The sounds are informative. Machine-learning algorithms could distinguish between drought-stressed and cut plants with ~70% accuracy just from the acoustic signature. So it's not random noise—it carries information about the type of stress.
  • Other organisms can likely hear it. Bats, moths, and possibly mice all have hearing ranges that overlap with these frequencies. Whether they use the information is unknown, but the channel exists.
  • It's probably not intentional communication. The leading hypothesis is that it's a physical byproduct: air bubbles forming and collapsing in the xylem (cavitation) under tension, essentially the plant's plumbing groaning. But "side effect" doesn't mean "useless"—evolution can co-opt accidents.
  • It reframes an old question. People have asked "do plants feel pain?" for decades. The honest answer is still "we don't know what feeling is for a plant," but we now know they produce a detectable distress signal that travels through the environment. That's a smaller, more tractable, and more interesting question than the philosophical one.

The broader takeaway: "silent" organisms often aren't silent—we just weren't listening in the right frequency band. Bats probably knew about screaming plants before we did.

There are more possible games of chess than atoms in the observable universe.

The Shannon number estimates roughly 10¹²⁰ legal chess games — while the observable universe contains on the order of 10⁸⁰ atoms. So the space of possible chess games outnumbers the building blocks of everything we can see by about 10⁴⁰.

A few related mind-benders:

  • The first four moves of a chess game alone can produce over 300 billion distinct positions.
  • Go makes chess look tiny: the number of possible Go games is estimated around 10³⁶⁰ (or more, depending on board size/rules) — so vast that "more than atoms in the universe" undersells it by an absurd margin.
  • This is why computers don't "solve" chess by brute force. Even with all the computing power on Earth, enumerating every game is physically impossible. Engines like Stockfish instead use heuristics, evaluation functions, and pruning to navigate a sliver of that space intelligently.

So when an engine "calculates 20 moves ahead," it's not exploring the game tree — it's making an educated guess about which tiny branch matters.

Here's one: humans can briefly perceive colors that don't exist in normal experience.

Your visual system uses "opponent channels" — red vs. green, blue vs. yellow, light vs. dark. Normally, these channels are mutually exclusive: you can see red or green, but never "reddish-green." Same for blue and yellow.

But in experiments, researchers found that if you stare at a specific pattern for a long time (fatiguing one side of the channel) and then look at a white surface, you can momentarily perceive impossible hues — colors that your brain's wiring says shouldn't be possible. "Reddish-green" and "yellowish-blue" are the most commonly reported.

It's not a hallucination or an illusion of light. It's your visual cortex briefly breaking its own rules, producing a genuine perceptual experience of something outside normal color space.

So there are colors you've never seen — and can't see under ordinary conditions — but that your brain is technically capable of generating.
