Here's one most people haven't heard: Earth once had natural nuclear reactors.

In 1972, scientists analyzing uranium ore from a mine in Oklo, Gabon, noticed the uranium-235 was slightly depleted — as if it had already been through a reactor. The explanation was wild: about 1.7 billion years ago, natural uranium was ~3% U-235 (roughly the enrichment of modern reactor fuel), and groundwater seeping through the deposit acted as a neutron moderator, kick-starting self-sustaining fission chain reactions.

The "reactors" ran in roughly 30-minute bursts, on and off, for hundreds of thousands of years — heat boiled the water away, reactions paused, water seeped back, reactions resumed. It's the only known place on Earth where this ever happened, and it was discovered because some numbers on a lab report didn't quite add up.

Two bonuses, free of charge:

  • Woolly mammoths were still alive when the Great Pyramid was being built — a population survived on Wrangel Island until around 2000 BC.
  • The Sahara fertilizes the Amazon: millions of tons of dust cross the Atlantic each year, delivering the phosphorus the rainforest's soil lacks.

Here's one with a decent shot at being new to you:

In 1972, physicists analyzing uranium ore from a mine in Oklo, Gabon, found something that shouldn't exist: the uranium-235 was slightly depleted, exactly like spent fuel from a manmade reactor. After ruling out contamination and error, they landed on a wild conclusion — the deposit had been a natural nuclear reactor, and it had gone critical roughly 1.7 billion years ago.

Back then, natural uranium was about 3% U-235, roughly the enrichment level used in modern reactors. Groundwater seeping through the ore acted as a neutron moderator, and the thing just... ran. It cycled on and off for hundreds of thousands of years — something like 30 minutes of fission, then a couple of hours off while the water boiled away and recondensed — before finally burning out. Several reactor zones were eventually identified at the site.

It couldn't happen today, because U-235 decays faster than U-238, leaving natural uranium too lean at 0.72%.

So nuclear reactors were invented twice: once by humans, and once by geology, about 1.7 billion years earlier.
