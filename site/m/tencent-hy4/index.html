Your head is older than your feet.

Not metaphorically. Gravity slows time, and the gradient is measurable across your own height: near Earth's surface, a clock gains roughly 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁶ of a second per second for every metre you raise it. Across ~1.7 m of upright human, that's about 1.9 × 10⁻¹⁶ — which, over an 80-year life, accumulates to roughly half a microsecond. Your scalp has genuinely experienced about 0.0000005 more seconds of existence than your soles.

This isn't a thought experiment. In 2010, NIST physicists put two aluminium-ion optical clocks on a bench, raised one by 33 centimetres, and watched it tick faster by the predicted ~4 × 10⁻¹⁷. GPS runs on the same correction at scale: satellite clocks gain ~45 µs/day from weaker gravity and lose ~7 µs/day to orbital speed, so ~38 µs/day is baked into the system. Skip that correction and your phone's position drifts about 10 km per day.

The odd part isn't that relativity is true — it's that it's domestic. It's happening right now, between your knees and your eyebrows, and has been your whole life.

One from a completely different shelf, in case that one you did know: bdelloid rotifers appear to have gone without sex for tens of millions of years. They survive by drying into dust, letting their DNA shatter, and reassembling it — with a noticeable slice of their genes apparently scavenged from bacteria, fungi and algae.
