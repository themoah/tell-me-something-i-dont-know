You have never actually seen your own eyes move in a mirror.

If you stand in front of a mirror and shift your gaze from your left eye to your right eye, your eyes will seem to snap instantly into the new position. Even though someone standing next to you could easily watch them move, you will never catch them in motion.

This happens because of a neurological trick called saccadic masking. Whenever your eyes dart rapidly from one point to another (a movement called a saccade), your brain temporarily pauses visual processing so you don't get disoriented by the constant motion blur. To hide these tiny moments of blindness, your brain retroactively fills in the gap with the image it sees once your eyes land.

Because your eyes make these rapid jumps several times every second, your brain is constantly editing out tiny blackouts—meaning you spend a surprisingly large portion of your waking life effectively blind without ever realizing it.

(It’s also the reason why, when you glance at an analog clock, the very first tick of the second hand sometimes seems to last noticeably longer than the ones after it—your brain stretched the image back in time to cover the blur of your eye movement.)

Because of Einstein’s theory of general relativity, the Earth’s core is about 2.5 years younger than its surface.

Gravity doesn't just pull on objects; it warps time itself. The deeper you are in a gravitational well, the slower time ticks. The difference between the center of the Earth and the crust is minuscule—a clock at the core ticks only about three ten-billionths of a second slower per second than one on the surface.

However, over the 4.5 billion years since Earth formed, those tiny fractions have compounded into a two-and-a-half-year age gap.

(Bonus: The same physics applies to the Sun, which is so much more massive that its core is roughly 40,000 years younger than its surface.)

Humans weren't the first to build a nuclear reactor—nature beat us to it by about 1.7 billion years.

In 1972, French physicists examining uranium ore from a mine in Oklo, Gabon, noticed something baffling: the ore was missing a tiny fraction of its Uranium-235, the isotope used in nuclear fission. Normally, the ratio of U-235 is identical everywhere on Earth, on the Moon, and even in meteorites. The only way it could be depleted was if it had already been "burned" in a nuclear reaction.

Scientists eventually realized they had discovered the world's only known natural nuclear fission reactor.

Two billion years ago, natural U-235 was much more abundant than it is today (about 3% of uranium deposits—the same concentration used in modern enriched reactor fuel). In Oklo, a massive, concentrated deposit of this uranium happened to sit in porous rock. Whenever groundwater seeped into the rock, the water acted as a moderator, slowing down neutrons and triggering a self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction.

The heat from the reaction would eventually boil the water away, shutting the reactor down. Once the rock cooled, groundwater would seep back in, restarting the process.

This natural reactor cycled on and off like a radioactive geyser—running for about 30 minutes and cooling for two and a half hours—for hundreds of thousands of years, generating an average of about 100 kilowatts of power (roughly enough to run a few dozen toasters).
