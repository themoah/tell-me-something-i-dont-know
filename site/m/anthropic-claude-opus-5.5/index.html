The UK's tax year starts on April 6th because of a calendar change in 1752.

Until then, Britain used the Julian calendar, and the new year officially began on March 25th (Lady Day), a traditional date for settling debts and rents. In 1752, Britain switched to the Gregorian calendar and skipped 11 days: September 2nd was followed directly by September 14th.

Tax collectors didn't want to lose 11 days of revenue, so they moved the end of the tax year forward 11 days, to April 5th. Then in 1800, the Julian calendar would have had a leap day but the Gregorian didn't, so they shifted it one more day to April 6th.

Nobody ever bothered to change it back, so British taxes still run on a schedule set by an 18th-century calendar mismatch.
